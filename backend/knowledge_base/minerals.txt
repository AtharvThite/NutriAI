INTRODUCTION

Minerals are inorganic elements derived from the earth's crust and soil, taken up by plants or consumed by animals, and required by the human body to sustain structural integrity, maintain electrochemical gradients, and catalyze biochemical pathways. Unlike vitamins and macronutrients, minerals are simple chemical elements that cannot be broken down, synthesized, or degraded by heat, oxygen, or digestion. In human physiology, minerals are classified into macrominerals (required in amounts greater than 100 milligrams per day) and microminerals or trace elements (required in quantities ranging from micrograms to a few milligrams per day). Minerals serve as foundational architectural constituents of bones and teeth, regulate extracellular and intracellular fluid osmolarity, govern neuromuscular signal transmission, and form the active catalytic centers of metalloenzymes and transport proteins. In Indian dietary landscapes, ensuring adequate mineral bioavailability—particularly for iron, calcium, and zinc—is a vital public health priority due to the presence of natural dietary absorption inhibitors in plant staples.

IMPORTANT CONCEPTS

Classification of Dietary Minerals:

1. Macrominerals:
- Calcium (Ca): The most abundant mineral in the body; 99% is stored in bone and teeth as hydroxyapatite. The remaining 1% coordinates vascular contraction, muscle contraction, nerve transmission, and cellular signaling.
- Phosphorus (P): Integral to bone structure, cell membrane phospholipids, and high-energy phosphate bonds (ATP).
- Magnesium (Mg): A cofactor for over 300 enzymatic systems, including protein synthesis, blood glucose control, and blood pressure regulation.
- Electrolytes (Sodium, Potassium, Chloride): Regulate fluid balance, plasma volume, acid-base equilibrium, and the electrical membrane potential of cardiac and neural tissues. High sodium combined with low potassium intake is a recognized contributor to arterial hypertension.

2. Trace Elements (Microminerals):
- Iron (Fe): A core component of hemoglobin in red blood cells and myoglobin in muscle, responsible for oxygen transport and cellular electron transfer. Exists in food as heme iron (in meat, poultry, fish; high bioavailability, 15-35%) and non-heme iron (in plants, dairy, fortified foods; lower bioavailability, 2-10%, sensitive to inhibitors and enhancers).
- Zinc (Zn): Required for cellular metabolism, immune function, protein synthesis, wound healing, DNA synthesis, and taste acuity.
- Iodine (I): Essential constituent of thyroid hormones (thyroxine T4 and triiodothyronine T3) regulating basal metabolic rate and neural development.
- Selenium (Se), Copper (Cu), Chromium (Cr), Manganese (Mn), Molybdenum (Mo): Vital cofactors in antioxidant defense (glutathione peroxidase, superoxide dismutase) and macronutrient metabolism.

Bioavailability and Anti-Nutritional Factors:
The presence of minerals in food does not guarantee intestinal absorption. Plant matrices contain natural compounds—such as phytic acid (phytates) in whole grains and legumes, oxalic acid (oxalates) in spinach and beetroot, and polyphenols/tannins in tea and coffee—that chelate divalent mineral cations (Fe2+, Ca2+, Zn2+), forming insoluble complexes that resist absorption.

INDIAN FOOD EXAMPLES

Indian ingredients supply rich sources of essential minerals:

Calcium: Milk, curd (dahi), paneer, ragi (finger millet—which has by far the highest calcium content among cereals), sesame seeds (til), curry leaves (kadi patta), and tofu.

Iron: Whole pulses (kala chana, rajma, horse gram/kulthi), green leafy vegetables (fenugreek leaves, amaranth/chaulai, drumstick leaves), bajra (pearl millet), rice flakes (poha), jaggery (in small amounts), and organ meats, poultry, or fish.

Magnesium: Whole grains, millets, pumpkin seeds, almonds, cashews, peanuts, and green vegetables containing chlorophyll.

Potassium: Bananas, tender coconut water (nariyal pani, an excellent natural electrolyte source), potatoes, sweet potatoes, tomatoes, and pulses.

Zinc: Legumes, sesame seeds, pumpkin seeds, dairy foods, eggs, and seafood.

Iodine: Iodized table salt (the cornerstone of national iodine deficiency prevention), coastal seafood, and dairy products derived from iodine-supplemented livestock feed.

PRACTICAL APPLICATIONS

Overcoming mineral absorption inhibitors in Indian home kitchens is readily achievable through traditional culinary practices:

1. Soaking and Fermenting: Soaking whole legumes, dals, and grains in warm water for 6 to 12 hours activates endogenous plant phytases that degrade phytic acid. Fermenting batter for idlis and dosas further reduces phytates and enhances iron and zinc bioavailability.

2. Sprouting and Germination: Sprouting green moong, kala chana, or matki dramatically lowers phytate levels, rendering bound minerals significantly more absorbable.

3. Strategic Meal Timing of Enhancers and Inhibitors: Avoid consuming strong black tea or coffee during or immediately following meals, as tannins and polyphenols can reduce non-heme iron absorption by up to 60-70%. Conversely, pair iron-rich meals with ascorbic acid (vitamin C)—such as adding lemon juice, tomatoes, or amla—to enhance non-heme iron uptake.

4. Roasting and Cooking Techniques: Lightly roasting oilseeds (such as til or flaxseed) reduces anti-nutritional factors and improves the digestibility of contained minerals.

COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS

A prevalent belief is that spinach (palak) is an ideal, highly absorbable source of calcium and iron. While spinach contains substantial gross quantities of both elements, it is simultaneously rich in oxalic acid, which binds tightly to calcium and iron, resulting in poor human intestinal absorption (less than 5% for calcium). Ragi and dairy are far more bioavailable calcium sources.

Another common misconception is that using unrefined pink Himalayan rock salt or black salt (kala namak) is inherently superior and allows unlimited consumption. While rock salt contains minor trace minerals, it is chemically over 95% sodium chloride. Excess intake carries the same hypertensive cardiovascular risks as standard salt, and standard rock salt often lacks mandatory potassium iodate fortification.

A third myth is that cooking in iron pots (lohe ki kadhai) provides dangerous, uncontrolled iron levels. Cooking acidic food preparations (such as tomato-based gravies) in seasoned iron cookware does leach modest amounts of non-heme iron into the food, which can safely contribute to dietary iron intake in iron-deficient populations.

LIMITATIONS AND UNCERTAINTY

Estimating actual mineral absorption from simple food records involves notable uncertainty. Food composition tables list total chemical mineral content, but the net physiological absorption depends heavily on the individual's current nutritional status (e.g., an iron-depleted individual upregulates intestinal DMT1 transporters to absorb higher percentages of non-heme iron) and the precise balance of inhibitors (phytates, oxalates) and enhancers (ascorbic acid, animal tissue factor) present in that specific meal.

Accurate evaluation requires contextual knowledge of food processing methods and verified composition values from comprehensive sources like Anuvaad.

SUMMARY

Minerals are inorganic elements essential for skeletal structure, fluid and electrolyte balance, oxygen transport, and enzyme function. While Indian plant foods provide substantial quantities of iron, calcium, magnesium, and zinc, absorption is modulated by natural inhibitors like phytates and oxalates. Traditional preparation methods—such as soaking, sprouting, fermenting, and pairing with vitamin C—substantially improve mineral bioavailability.
